## Research findings
No dedicated research-only day today (not a Phase 3/Sunday slot), but the companion blog post required its own research pass. Beyond the sources cited above, a few notes worth keeping for future on-demand/marketplace work:
- Most vendor "on-demand app testing" content is marketing-driven with few independently verifiable numbers (e.g. a commonly-repeated "75% of QA teams adopted self-healing testing" stat traces only to a testing-vendor's own blog — treated as unverified and excluded from the post).
- Uber's own engineering blog (`eng.uber.com`) and developer docs (`developer.uber.com`) are the strongest primary sources available for this vertical — worth checking first for any future ride-hailing/dispatch-adjacent content rather than relying on third-party summaries.
- `dontkillmyapp.com` is a solid, citable community source for OEM-specific Android background-restriction behavior (useful beyond this one post — relevant to any future mobile-notification-reliability content).

## LinkedIn draft
The companion post went live today, so here's a ready-to-post draft (draft only — the owner posts it manually; this doesn't replace the Saturday founder-post rule in RUNBOOK section 0, which is separate):

> Most "mobile app testing checklist" posts stop at devices and OS versions. On-demand and marketplace apps have a second layer most checklists skip: two apps, a matching engine, and a payments ledger that all have to agree on the same live state.
>
> A few things worth testing on purpose, not by accident:
> → Driver/provider status needs to reach the matching engine within seconds — Uber's own docs flag this explicitly, because a delay can trigger a timeout.
> → GPS accuracy varies by device and degrades in dense urban areas — exactly where demand concentrates. Simulators won't catch this; real devices will.
> → A push notification can fail silently because of the *device*, not the app. Several Android OEMs ship battery management that quietly blocks background notifications — documented per-manufacturer by dontkillmyapp.com.
> → Large platforms treat peak-load testing as a recurring drill, not a one-time pre-launch check (Uber runs theirs weekly).
