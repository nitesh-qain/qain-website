**The follow-the-sun QA handoff: what actually makes the protocol work**

"Follow-the-sun" gets used as a buzzword for any arrangement where a dev team and a QA team sit in different time zones. That's not wrong, but it skips past the part that actually matters: time-zone offset by itself doesn't make this work or fail. The handoff discipline does.

Here's the pattern, stripped down to the parts that are load-bearing:

**1. A short daily sync in the overlap window — even if it's only an hour.** This isn't a status meeting. Its one job is triage: what's "done enough to test" as of right now, and what from yesterday's testing needs a decision before anyone logs off. Skip this and the handoff degenerates into a one-way inbox — dev throws code over the wall, QA throws bugs back, and nobody's actually talking.

**2. QA tests what was actually committed that day, not a day-old branch.** This sounds obvious written down, but it's the single easiest discipline to let slip. If QA is testing against yesterday's code because today's branch wasn't cut cleanly, the team loses the entire point of the overnight window — devs come back to results about code they've already half-forgotten, instead of a tested, current backlog.

**3. The handoff gets written down, not just said once in a sync call.** A short end-of-day note — what shipped, what's risky, what to watch — survives a time-zone gap in a way a verbal handoff doesn't. The team on the other side of the gap wasn't in the room; if the only record of a decision is "we talked about it," it's effectively unrecorded. This is the cheapest fix in the whole list and the one most teams skip first when they're busy.

**4. Separate "time-zone lag" from "distance lag."** A lot of what gets blamed on the time-zone offset is actually a documentation habit problem that would exist even on a single-timezone distributed team. Fixing the habit removes most of the pain before you touch the schedule at all.

**Where this breaks down.** If a team has close to zero overlap *and* no handoff discipline, the day genuinely feels one cycle behind, permanently. That's a real failure mode, not a strawman — it's what "outsourcing across time zones" skeptics are usually picturing when they object to the model sight unseen.

**Where it doesn't break down.** A 3.5–5.5 hour offset — roughly what a UK or EU team sees against an India-based team, or what a Japan/Korea-to-India pairing looks like — gives a full working-day overlap window if the team actually uses it for triage rather than letting it default to "nobody's awake to talk." That's closer to synchronous collaboration than the "time zones" objection usually assumes going in. A larger offset, like a US-to-India pairing with near-zero natural overlap, is where the overnight handoff itself becomes the main value: devs end their day, QA works through the night against what was actually shipped, and the team is back to tested code — not a backlog — the next morning. The handoff discipline matters more as the offset grows, not less.

None of the four practices above require outsourcing anything. They apply the same way to an in-house distributed team split across offices. The only thing outsourcing changes is that the offset is usually bigger and the incentive to get the discipline right is usually higher, because there's no hallway to fall back on when the written handoff is thin.
